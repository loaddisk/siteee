##Stars & Cancer Cells##
07/06/2026

The more one pays attention, the more everything funnels into a single word. Not how, not when, not even what - but, why? 

It is the word beneath all other words, the root from which every protest and every prayer grows. ‘Why’ has become the most important question. Not merely for philosophers or priests, but for anyone who has watched something precious break, or something undeserved arrive at their door.

It is a word that demands examination and there is one question in particular that has refused to leave me:

If the universe is wise, if it carries us where we ought to go, if surrender is a form of trust rather than defeat, then why does it permit what it permits? Why do the undeserving suffer? Why are the gentle buried young? Why does disease choose a child as readily as a tyrant? And for life of integrity to end in ruin? What lesson is so valuable that it requires such tuition?

For what reason does a star exhaust itself in fire? What lesson is taught by a tumor multiplying in the dark? Nature offers no explanation. It simply proceeds.

And the longer I sat with these questions, the more I began to understand that perhaps I was asking the wrong thing. I was asking nature to justify itself, as though it owed me an account of its decisions.

Yet the stars do not ask whether their light will illuminate a saint or a thief. The rain does not choose between the grateful and the bitter. The sea does not spare the virtuous sailor from the storm. The earth receives every body without inquiry.

This is not evidence of cruelty, it may simply be evidence of impartiality.

The mistake may be in imagining that the universe exists to spare us pain. We enter life as citizens of a world governed by laws, not preferences. The oak is not grown for comfort but for strength. Fire does not ask what it burns before it tests the metal. Winter does not seek permission before it arrives.

Perhaps the question is not why suffering exists, but why we expected exemption from it.

This does not erase grief. It does not make loss any less painful, nor disease any less terrible. A philosophy that asks us not to mourn, would be a philosophy at war with human nature. We grieve because we love. We suffer because we have been attached to things whose departure was always beyond our control.

But grief and outrage are not the same thing.

To grieve is human. To insist that reality should have arranged itself differently is to wage a war that cannot be won.

The stars burn. Cells divide. Bodies flourish and fail. Nature conducts its affairs with a consistency that can appear merciless only when measured against our wishes.

Yet there is something strangely liberating in this. If suffering is not a personal sentence, then neither is it a personal injustice. We are not singled out by fate. We are participants in the same conditions that govern everything that lives.

Stars and cancer cells share a quality that unsettles us: neither asks permission. Both simply obey their nature.

And perhaps wisdom begins when we learn to do the same, not by becoming indifferent, but by accepting that reality is under no obligation to conform to our hopes. Our task is not to demand a gentler universe, but to become equal to the one that already exists.

Stars and cancer cells.
