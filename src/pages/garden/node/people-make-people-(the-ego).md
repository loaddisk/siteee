You’ve probably heard of the flea effect: a classic illustration of why animals (and people) stop trying to escape a bad situation (which may not be realised), even after the barrier holding them back is completely gone. In psychology, the closest parallel is learned helplessness.

Fleas can easily leap a hundred times their own height. Place them in an open jar, and they spring straight to freedom. Place a lid upon that jar, and they will jump, strike the ‘ceiling’ and fall back down. After a few days, remove the lid and they still will not leap out. They have trained their own nature to stop below an invisible line, though nothing remains to stop them. 

Worse still, if they breed inside that open jar, their young watch them jump only halfway up and imitate their restraint. Though the lid is long gone and the young have never struck a ceiling, they remain prisoners - bound entirely by the limits they learned from the crowd around them.

And what, you may ask, has this to do with the ego?

I have spent much of this past year, and the one preceding it, discovering that I, too, have been living in a jar. The more uncomfortable discovery was that I had mistaken its walls for the boundaries of my own nature. 

Every experience, every judgement, every expectation, every disappointment had contributed another grain to the creation of the glass. Some grains were built by my upbringing, others by the opinions of those around me and others learned reactions and actions. Eventually, the construction became so familiar that I ceased to recognise it as a construction at all.

![[IMG_0161.jpeg]]

(A close up of a grain of sand)

I looked in the mirror and called the result myself.

I learned that this jar isn’t permanent, this jar can be modified and designed to your liking. Doing things without reason and never asking the questions that hit the gear pedals: “Why?”, “Why do I?” “why did I?” and “why do I keep doing?”

![[IMG_0157.png]]

It was these very questions that started speaking to me, that started to keep me awake when I’d rather be asleep.

Whatever my eyes saw is immediately seized upon by the ego, triggering an automatic reaction. I noticed I had these knee-jerk feelings driven entirely by old habit. It is a physical reflex, almost like flinching at a sudden noise. And I am not talking about any sort of grand tragedy (though it can apply) but I’ve noticed the mind reveals its chains in the smallest, most ridiculous trifles.

Here are a couple of examples: 

I. I saw a guy wearing jeans at the gym, my immediate response was irritation. My mind had scarcely registered the sight before it had delivered its verdict: What an idiot.

But upon what grounds?

His clothing had deprived me of nothing. His decision had neither injured me nor interfered with my affairs. And yet here I was: annoyed.

Why? Because I had established an unwritten rule: people should wear gym wear at the gym. Anyone who departed from this expectation was, in my estimation, deserving of judgement.

Observe how readily the ego manufactures offences. It creates a law, forgets that it created the law, and then condemns others for failing to obey it.

II. On another occasion, my sister remarked, “Everyone has pissed me off today.”

Immediately, I became defensive.

“What have I done?” I asked, proceeding to assemble a list of reasons why I could not possibly have been responsible for her frustration.

I was preparing a defence against an accusation that had never been made.

Only afterwards did I recognise the absurdity. She had not necessarily been speaking about me at all. And even if she had, her feelings would not automatically constitute an indictment of my character.

Yet the ego had heard a general statement and interpreted it as a personal attack. It did not first seek understanding; it sought protection. It would rather defend an imaginary accusation than patiently discover whether an accusation existed.

Such is the fragility of an identity that requires constant confirmation.

We suffer not only from what others say, but from the meanings we hastily attach to their words.

But what governs these reactions?

I do not believe we can always prevent the first impression from entering the mind. An emotion may arise before reason has had the opportunity to intervene. We cannot command the wind to cease blowing, nor should we condemn ourselves merely because it has disturbed the water. Thoughts cannot be dictated to exist or not exist. 

But we may decide whether to follow it.

There is a distinction, often neglected, between experiencing an impression and consenting to it. The first may be involuntary; the second is where our responsibility begins.

A thought may present itself, but it need not become our belief. An emotion may arise, but it need not become our conduct. We need not give every passing impression the authority of a command.

The mind is much like a radio. We may not always choose the first sound that enters it, but we can choose which station we continue to listen to. There is no reason to remain tuned to static merely because it was the first thing we heard.

![[IMG_3353.gif]]

Our habits have taught us certain equations. We have learned that two plus two must equal four, and that when a particular circumstance arises, a particular reaction must follow. But many of these equations are not laws of nature. They are merely familiar conclusions, repeated so often that we have stopped examining them.

And what has been learned may be unlearned.

Imagine a path through a field of wheat. The first path is clear because it has been walked a thousand times. You need not think about where to place your feet; the route has become automatic.

Now suppose you wish to walk elsewhere.

The new path is difficult. The wheat is tall, the ground unfamiliar, and every step requires deliberate effort. You will occasionally turn back towards the old route, not because it is better, but because it is easier.

This is precisely how habit maintains its authority.

To establish a new way of thinking, one must continue along the unfamiliar path until it, too, becomes familiar. The old route will not disappear simply because we have recognised its existence. It must be abandoned through repeated acts of deliberate choice.

And there will be failures.

But why should we regard a failure as the final verdict? To stumble whilst learning a new path is no disgrace and no failure. Would you become angry with your legs because they tire on unfamiliar ground? Would you abandon the journey because the first steps were difficult?

We must extend to the mind the same patience we would extend to the body. Discipline does not demand perfection; it demands persistence consistence.

The objective is not to become a person who never experiences irritation, insecurity, jealousy or defensiveness. This would be asking for a human to cease being human. 
The objective is to become a person who can recognise these things without becoming their servant.

For the greatest victory is not to command the world, nor to ensure that others behave according to our expectations, it is to cease being governed by every impression the world places before us, by every thought that comes to mind.

And perhaps this brings us back to the fleas.

We are creatures shaped by other creatures. We inherit their habits, their fears, their prejudices and their expectations. Before we are old enough to question the world, we have already begun imitating it. We learn what to admire, what to resent, what to represent what to fear and, most dangerously, what we believe ourselves capable of achieving.

People make people.

But this is not permeant. 

People make people. The question is whether we shall remain copies of the people who made us or become the authors of what comes next?
